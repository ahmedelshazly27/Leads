# Qoves sells explanation, not measurement precision

Qoves (qoves.com) now sells one thing: a **$150-a-year membership that buys one human-reviewed facial analysis per year**. It covers a 169-item assessment catalogue across 12 facial regions, six composite score "pillars", a phased non-surgical protocol in which each step is costed and timed, AI "after" projections, a letter from the team, and dashboard chat with a care team. Delivery takes **up to 28 days**, and refunds stop once the customer uploads photos. Paid add-ons cover 48-hour delivery and surgical recommendations, and a reported hairstyle add-on brings the full spend to about $435. The analysis is deep in coverage and shallow in method. It defines dozens of anthropometric measurements and compares each to sex-specific ranges, which come mostly from Western clinical textbooks plus a handful of ethnicity rows, Middle Eastern among them. It publishes no validation, no measurement error, no stratified norms with sample sizes and no identities for the reviewers. Buyers value the long, perception-focused, feature-by-feature write-up and the candour about what is realistic. They dislike the wait, the refund terms, the generic feel and the "after" image. Journalists and researchers have criticised the company consistently since 2021 for bias, thin science and body-dysmorphia risk. A Gulf competitor that is science-backed and human-reviewed has to match the catalogue breadth, the narrative depth and the costed protocol. It can beat Qoves on regional norms, turnaround, transparency, safety screening and refunds. It should avoid Qoves' surgical upsell, its inconsistent claims and the age-distorting projections. The prioritised build list is in the final section.

**How to read the evidence.** Three grades of source are used throughout. **[F]** marks Qoves' own live pages, fetched and read directly on 8 November 2026: the homepage and its FAQ structured data, terms, checkout code, about-us, man-protocol, measurement pages, guides and comparison pages. **[B]** marks first-hand buyer testimony: two buyer accounts the project owner supplied (not independently verified), plus named first-person reviews. **[S]** marks second-hand material: search-engine snippets of Trustpilot, Reddit, looksmax.org, the Guardian and app stores, and especially "Qoves review" pages run by competing face-analysis products (SoftMaxx, RealWorldAppeal, TheFaceAnalysis, TheFaceReport). Those competitors have a commercial interest. Reddit, Trustpilot, YouTube, the Wayback Machine and the 2026 Guardian feature could not be read directly.

## One $150 membership replaced a five-tier PDF catalogue

**What is sold today [F].** Qoves' comparison pages describe a "yearly membership at $150, cancellable any time" ([Qoves vs Umax](https://www.qoves.com/insights/qoves-vs-umax)), and another of its own articles says the $150 covers the measurement, a human reviewer checking what the software found, and the written report ([How a glow-up actually happens](https://www.qoves.com/insights/how-a-glow-up-actually-happens)). The homepage pricing block anchors the price against a struck-through "$10,000". It promises "One analysis per year. No hidden fees." and "Cancel or pause anytime". The live price is injected client-side from an API, with USD and EUR formatting ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). The membership includes the complete facial analysis, a personalised glow-up protocol, biometric scores with tracking, before/after visualisations, a "protocol letter" from the team, and dashboard messaging with a care team. Qoves says the support team responds within five minutes, answers beauty questions within 24 hours and is "always real people" ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)).

**Add-ons sit outside the headline price.** The checkout code [F] contains a "One-time add-ons" step with two IDs, `express-delivery` and `comprehensive-report`. Its copy says Express delivers only the main report and protocol within 48 hours, and that express orders "with surgical recommendations may take up to 7 working days". Payment runs through Stripe ([Qoves checkout](https://www.qoves.com/welcome/checkout)). The checkout code shows no prices. Competitor sources report **Express $80, Surgical Recommendations $180 and Hairstyle Suggestions $25 (five styles)**, a realistic total of **$150–$410**, and an all-in figure of **$435** [S] ([SoftMaxx pricing](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-pricing); [RealWorldAppeal](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-review)). One buyer [B] bought the base report plus the surgical and hair add-ons, which confirms that both upsells exist in practice.

**Turnaround and refunds [F].** The FAQ promises results "up to 28 days" after photo upload ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). Qoves' Head of Strategy turned this into a slogan in BeautyMatter: the report takes 28 days rather than 28 seconds ([BeautyMatter](https://www.beautymatter.com/articles/the-slopification-of-beauty)). Under the terms, updated 15 May 2026, a customer gets a **full refund only if they have not yet submitted photos or the questionnaire**, and must ask within 90 days. There is no refund once the customer uploads or work begins. Express fees are non-refundable once any part of the order has been delivered, and revisions go through the care team ([Terms and conditions](https://www.qoves.com/legal/terms-and-conditions)). The terms also limit the service to adults, say it is "not a replacement for professional medical advice", and state that Qoves does not guarantee the accuracy of all analyses [F].

**The product history explains much of the confusion in reviews.** Until about mid-2026 the v1 store sold one-off PDF reports. These were a **Facial Morph Report ($100)**, a **Preliminary Aesthetics Report ($150, about 10 pages)**, a **Tertiary report ($250, 16 pages, 32 measurements)**, a **Comprehensive report ($300, 24 pages, 38 measurements)** and a **Hairline Design Report ($250, 24 pages)** ([v1 Comprehensive](https://v1.qoves.com/product/comprehensive-aesthetics-report/); [v1 Tertiary](https://v1.qoves.com/product/tertiary-aesthetics-report/); [v1 Hairline](https://v1.qoves.com/product/hairline-report/)). There was also a Style Lookbook offering "fashion and styling advice for your face & body", whose price was not found [S] ([SoftMaxx](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-studio)). Buyers quote other prices along the way: $300 in July 2023 ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AMiE3_UavZk)), $160 and $175 in early-to-mid 2026 ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNKqMzlSXD4)), and $330 for a 30-page report with the surgical upgrade (Guardian, via [Streamline Feed](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/paid-face-scans-sell-science-that-experts-say-cannot-exist)) [S]. Before any of this, in 2021, Qoves ran a free AI "Facial Assessment Tool" that returned a report card of predicted flaws ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/03/05/1020133/ai-algorithm-rate-beauty-score-attractive-face/)). The arc runs from a free AI score, to tiered PDF dossiers, to a recurring membership with post-click upsells. The headline price fell by half, and revenue now depends on renewals and add-ons. **Qoves has no body-analysis product today.** The retired lookbook is the only trace of one, so the body lane is open.

## A 169-item catalogue built on mostly Western textbook norms

**Catalogue breadth by region [F].** The homepage lists "160+ aesthetic assessments", each defined on the page. Counting the items gives **169**, distributed as shown below ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). This catalogue sets the breadth bar a competitor has to clear.

| Eyebrows | 14 | Brow position and arch (brow apex angle, elevation) |
| Eyes | 26 | Limbal ring visibility, scleral colour, canthal tilt, almondness |
| Nose | 17 | Tip rotation and projection, dorsum, alar width |
| Lips | 16 | Upper:lower lip ratio, Cupid's bow |
| Cheeks | 13 | Malar prominence, midface volume |

**Underneath sits a public measurement library [F].** It has about 65 pages, and every one follows the same template: definition, landmarks used, why the measurement matters, men's, women's and "typical" range cards, a by-demographic table with a source on each row, and an FAQ ([Qoves measurements](https://www.qoves.com/insights/measurements)). The ranges are specific. Gonial angle is 118–130° for men and 118–126° for women ([gonial angle](https://www.qoves.com/insights/measurements/gonial-angle)). Canthal tilt is 0 to +4° for men and +2 to +8° for women. Facial width-to-height ratio is 1.7–2.1 for men, citing Lefevre 2013. Interpupillary distance is 60–68 mm for men and 58–65 mm for women. The nasolabial page is the most careful. It gives a "healthy" 90–110° and population means ranging from 97.9° in Turkish males to 104.3° in Han Chinese males, and it concedes that there is no single global ideal ([nasolabial angle](https://www.qoves.com/insights/measurements/nasolabial-angle)). The sources are author and year only, with no DOIs. Naini's *Facial Aesthetics* and Farkas dominate, and the demographic rows cover East Asian (Korean CBCT), Black American, nasal types, **Middle Eastern**, Hispanic/Mestizo and older adults. Every page carries the same "Reviewed October 6, 2026" stamp, and no reviewer is named. All of this points to **norms that are largely Caucasian clinical-textbook values with token ethnicity rows, not systematic mean ± SD by ancestry, sex and age.** A separate set of "evidence-based guides" covers averageness, symmetry, dimorphism, neoteny, adiposity, harmony and proportionality. These are better credentialed: the author is an Oxford psychology-research MSc, the reviewer is Chief Scientist Macken Murphy, and the proportionality guide explicitly rejects the rigid golden ratio [F].

**How results are expressed [F].** The marketing shows six score pillars: **Proportion, Dimorphism, Averageness, Neoteny, Contrast and Symmetry**. Dashboard tiles show Femininity (">90 ideal"), Averageness, Symmetry (>75), Homogeneity (>75), Proportionality (>80) and Visual Age, along with a year-over-year chart comparing "with" and "without" Qoves recommendations. Report copy states each value against a demographic band. The site's example compares a curvature of 0.23 with a demographic average of 0.21, inside a "25–32 White Female 0.18–0.26" band ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). One creator [B/S] was told they were at "49/100 of their potential" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNKqMzlSXD4)). That suggests a headline potential score exists even though the marketing does not show one.

**The protocol is the strongest product idea [F].** It is phased. The examples are "key vitality signals" in months 0–1, "basic facial proportions" in months 1–2 and "balancing dimorphism" in months 2–4. Each action lists its cost, frequency, time to result and difficulty; one example is 0.5% retinol at $35–55, used nightly, with results after 8+ weeks. Each change comes with several alternatives, a list of recommended products with risk levels, and an itemised budget ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/); [Qoves vs Umax](https://www.qoves.com/insights/qoves-vs-umax)). The "man protocol" page lays out a six-step logic: health signals, then thirds and fifths, then calibrating masculinity, then feature by feature, then "uniqueness and charm", then maintenance ([Man protocol](https://www.qoves.com/man-protocol)).

**Human versus machine [F/B].** Qoves claims every analysis is reviewed by specialists. Its FAQ admits it uses "tools like Claude" to articulate findings, while proprietary models do the analysis ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). It names ten advisory doctors, among them Gary Linkov, Jonathan Zelken and Lara Devgan, plus five full-time scientists ([About us](https://www.qoves.com/about-us)). It does not say who reviews a given report or what their qualifications are. One buyer [B] inferred human authorship from typos and double spaces. A sponsored Substack reviewer instead saw "heavy evidence of AI" ([Uncynical](https://uncynical.substack.com/p/i-tried-qoves-which-isnt-a-looksmaxxing)). The likely pipeline is automated landmarking and measurement, LLM-assisted drafting and a human pass, but that is an inference. Qoves has published **no accuracy or validation data** for its landmarking or scores.

**Projections are a weak point.** The "Remorph" engine produces before/after images [S] ([Trustpilot business profile](https://fi.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com)). Seven of 11 reviewers in one tally discussed the morph, and "looks like a different person" is a common complaint [S] ([RealWorldAppeal Reddit verdict](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-reddit-verdict)). One supplied buyer [B] said the projection "made me look older". Qoves' own content warns that general-purpose image generators alter unrelated facial traits ([Remio](https://www.remio.ai/post/qoves-facial-analysis-turns-beauty-scores-into-treatment-plans-but-measurement-i)), and its morphs show the same fault.

## Pay first, then wait four weeks: the customer journey

**The site is a single-offer conversion page [F].** The navigation has five items: Why Glow-up, How it works, FAQ, Login and "Start my plan". The homepage then runs through a long sequence. It opens on the hero "Improve your looks without surgery" with "Join 80,000+ people", followed by an As Seen In bar and before/projection sliders. Next come about 27 cited stats on how attractiveness affects outcomes, an "old way vs new way" contrast and the 160+ marker analysis. Further down are the protocol timeline ("450+ methods"), an ethnicity-aware block, biometrics and tracking, the full assessment catalogue, the advisors, the technology section (521 points, six pillars) and a "Reviewed by Humans" section. The page closes with transformations, testimonials, a three-step how-it-works, pricing, chat, the letter, lifetime tracking, a nine-tab FAQ and footer disclaimers ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). The visual language reads as "lab/biotech" rather than beauty. Type is PP Neue Montreal with a Zagma display or mono accent. Colours are slate-teal (#233137) and grey-teal (#758084, #9aaeb5) on off-white (#f2f2f2). The layout uses rounded cards, gradient panels and a metallic footer wordmark ([Fudge design capture](https://design.withfudge.com/share/qoves.com-design)).

**Journey [F].** The sequence starts with email and marketing consent, then add-ons, then Stripe payment. The customer then creates an account, completes the questionnaire and uploads photos in the browser, waits up to 28 days, and reads the report in a web dashboard with chat. There is no native app, and the site says it works on desktop, tablet and mobile browsers ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). Customers pay before seeing any value, and there is no free tier or preview [S] ([TheFaceAnalysis](https://thefaceanalysis.com/guides/qoves-review)).

**Intake [F/B].** The site says the questionnaire covers demographics, lifestyle and goals. It also asks which kinds of recommendation the customer will not accept and what cosmetic procedures they have had, and it personalises by ethnicity, age, location, diet, climate, stress, sleep, budget, ageing and cultural standards ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). One buyer [B] counted **about 22–23 questions**. These included multi-select ancestry, aesthetic reference faces such as a K-pop idol, and hair characteristics such as natural frizz. The Guardian reported that the adults-only gate and the self-reported body-image question **can be circumvented** [S] ([Streamline Feed](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/paid-face-scans-sell-science-that-experts-say-cannot-exist)).

**Photo angles are inconsistent.** The site asks for **six photos at "six specific angles"**, and its pose widget shows front, right side, left side and right quarter [F] ([Qoves homepage](https://www.qoves.com/)). One buyer [B] described front, 45° and 90° views. A Medium reviewer listed front, both sides, three-quarter, smiling and top of head, which comes to eight shots [B] ([David Alex, Medium](https://meetdavidalex.medium.com/qoves-full-review-bf17999832fd)). A Qoves comparison page refers to **nine photos** [F] ([Qoves vs Perfect Corp](https://www.qoves.com/insights/qoves-vs-perfect-corp)). The guidance asks for natural light, no makeup and no iPhone front camera [B] ([David Alex, Medium](https://meetdavidalex.medium.com/qoves-full-review-bf17999832fd)). No source shows live capture validation, pose checks or rejection of bad photos.

**Report experience.** The 2026 deliverable sits in a dashboard with chat [F]. Whether it still exports to PDF is unknown. Older reports were editorial PDF dossiers titled "Aesthetics Report, Comprehensive Edition". They had sections on cephalometric variability, periocular features, chin and lip profile, proportions, symmetry, dentofacial assessment, midface, jaw and profile, recommendations, references and an appendix [S] ([Scribd sample](https://www.scribd.com/document/554829284/QOVES-Sample-1-Comprehensive-Report); [looksmax.org PDF](https://i.looksmax.org/attachments/2025/05/4928024_Angel_Olavarria_Comprehensive_Report_1.pdf)). Readers report that the report takes almost an hour to read [S] ([SoftMaxx](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-review)).

**The app vacuum.** Qoves has no official app. An iOS listing called "Qoves: Face Analysis & Glow Up", published by APPAI LABS, looks like a renamed "FaceIQ" app selling at $9.99 a week or $49.99 a year, and its link to Qoves is unknown [S] ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/bo/app/qoves-face-analysis-glow-up/id6791538114?l=en-GB)). Third parties are borrowing the brand, which shows demand for an instant mobile entry point that Qoves does not offer.

## Buyers praise the explanation; forums dispute the value; the press doubts the science

**What buyers value [B].** Both supplied buyers valued the report less for its scores than for its explanation of how each feature, and each *combination* of features, is perceived. One example: outward cheekbones with a soft chin read as "sculpted feminine" rather than masculine. Buyers also valued detection of subtle traits they had never noticed, such as brow and chin asymmetry or a birthmark, together with concrete fixes like a side part to offset brow asymmetry and specific hyperpigmentation advice. One said the report was "honest about what was realistic and what wasn't", which increased their trust. Trustpilot snippets [S] repeat the same themes: "super detailed", "thorough and actionable", and praise for named, fast support agents ([nl.trustpilot](https://nl.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com); [ca.trustpilot](https://ca.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com)). Trustpilot rates Qoves at **about 4.6–4.8 across roughly 370–840 reviews**, depending on the snapshot [S] ([ie.trustpilot](https://ie.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com)).

**What they dislike [S/B].** The recurring complaints are the price for what arrives, the wait, a "generic" or formulaic feel, and unrealistic "after" images ([RealWorldAppeal](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-review)). Late delivery is common. One customer paid for express and was still waiting five days later; another overran by two weeks and never received the promised expedited report ([au.trustpilot](https://au.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com?page=3); [nz.trustpilot](https://nz.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com)). Customers also report **refunds refused, or granted only after a public review** ([uk.trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com?page=8)). Hairstyle suggestions did not suit some customers' actual appearance ([au.trustpilot](https://au.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com?page=8)). A supplied buyer [B] called it a "luxury service" that "might feel overpriced". On looksmax.org, sceptics say free community raters give similar feedback, while supporters say it is worth it "if you're seriously considering surgery" ([looksmax.org](https://looksmax.org/threads/qoves-facial-analysis-worth-it.1456357/); [looksmax.org](https://looksmax.org/threads/if-youre-serious-about-surgery-get-a-facial-analysis-from-qoves-before.449398/)). In one competitor's tally of 11 reviewers, 5 were positive (one of them sponsored), 3 mixed and 3 negative ([SoftMaxx](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-review)). Several influential "honest reviews" have conflicts of interest. One Medium reviewer later disclosed that she now works at Qoves, and the Substack reviewer received the service free ([Medium/Weiler](https://alessaweiler.medium.com/my-honest-qoves-review-i-tried-their-aesthetic-analysis-these-are-my-results-be7aa6d9b61f)).

**Critics have been consistent for five years.** MIT Technology Review found rating algorithms that penalise darker skin and wider noses and "display racist tendencies" ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/03/05/1020133/ai-algorithm-rate-beauty-score-attractive-face/)), and AIAAIC logged the 2021 tool as an AI incident ([AIAAIC](https://www.aiaaic.org/aiaaic-repository/ai-algorithmic-and-automation-incidents/qoves-ai-beauty-scoring)). A Guardian feature dated 5 August 2026, known here only through secondary summaries, reported several findings. Academics said the science is too thin for personalised recommendations, and a researcher Qoves cited did not endorse its use. One roughly 30-page report recommended rhinoplasty, lower blepharoplasty, canthopexy, chin implants and malarplasty. Qoves did not respond to the paper ([TheFaceAnalysis summary](https://thefaceanalysis.com/guides/qoves-review); [Streamline Feed](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/paid-face-scans-sell-science-that-experts-say-cannot-exist)). The most product-relevant evidence is a 2025 longitudinal study using two versions of the Qoves tool. Higher self-objectification went with lower self-esteem and more appearance-enhancement behaviour. **Softer "concerns" framing still produced negative reactions**, and the effects were stronger in women ([arXiv 2504.17055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17055)). Gentler wording alone does not make this category safe.

The overall picture is **satisfied buyers, a sceptical community and a hostile press**. Satisfied buyers get prompted to review, while doubters post on forums. A competitor will be judged in those forums and in the press, not on Trustpilot.

## A YouTube science channel funnels into a "without surgery" product

**The top of the funnel is QOVES Studio on YouTube [S].** The channel has about **1M subscribers, 333M lifetime views and 709 videos** as of May 2026, after adding about 93K subscribers over the previous ten months ([SpeakRJ](https://www.speakrj.com/audit/report/UCnlvhYDQLq4d_C3JYEVnWAQ/youtube)). The content is long-form explainers analysing celebrities, named traits such as hunter eyes and canthal tilt, and the science of attraction ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qoves)). The likely path runs from a free video to trait vocabulary, then to "find out yours" in a paid report. Organic TikTok uses the same mix. Its top posts are glow-up and asymmetry explainers, including one on attractiveness among Malaysian men, which shows Qoves already makes ethnicity-specific content ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/qovesstudio?lang=en)). Visible paid promotion consists of creator reviews on TikTok tagged #ad ([TikTok discover](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/qoves-review)). No ad library could be read, so the conventional ad mix is unknown.

**Mid-funnel is SEO [F].** The sitemap lists about 146 URLs. They include about 65 measurement pages, guides, 17 concern pages, 14 procedure pages, and comparison pages "QOVES vs" Umax, LooksMax AI, Haut.AI, YouCam, Perfect Corp, Face++, Azure Face and Amazon Rekognition ([Qoves vs LooksMax AI](https://www.qoves.com/insights/qoves-vs-looksmax-ai)). The positioning is **"app vs expert"**: a specialist-reviewed service set against instant scores with "no human involved". Qoves borrows press warnings about rating apps that harm young men to cast itself as the responsible option ([Qoves vs Umax](https://www.qoves.com/insights/qoves-vs-umax)). The homepage leans on social proof ("80,000+", "2M+ followers") and on a "4.7/5 · 600+ reviews" badge that names no platform ([Man protocol](https://www.qoves.com/man-protocol)). It also cites outcome statistics, and the Guardian traced at least one of them, on tipping, to a study used out of context [S].

**The voice is a dual register.** The research-lab voice talks of "biotechnology", "facial diagnostics", 521 landmarks and 450+ methods. The glow-up-coach voice says "improve your looks without surgery" and "max out your looks". Qoves asked one reviewer not to call it looksmaxxing ([Uncynical](https://uncynical.substack.com/p/i-tried-qoves-which-isnt-a-looksmaxxing)), yet its podcast is titled "Science-Based Advice to Max Out Your Looks" ([Chartable](https://chartable.com/podcasts/qoves-glow-up-science-based-advice-to-max-out-your-looks)). The claims carrying the most risk are the AI "after" images, which work like before/after advertising. Numeric attractiveness and "% of potential" scores and a surgical upsell from a provider that says it offers no licensed clinical services add to that risk. So do precision claims without validation. Middle Eastern regulators and ad platforms are likely to scrutinise each of these. This study did not research UAE or KSA rules, so a regulatory review is needed before launch.

## What a Gulf competitor must match, beat or avoid

This list pairs the evidence above with the competitor's stated position: science-backed, human-reviewed, Gulf-focused, face plus body. **P0** means required for launch parity or safety. **P1** is a differentiator for the first year. **P2** comes later. "Match" means equal Qoves, "Exceed" means beat it, and "Avoid" means do not copy it.

| # | Area | Qoves today (grade) | Competitor spec | Priority | Stance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Assessment catalogue by region | 169 items over 12 regions, from 8 general to 26 eye items [F] | At least 170 face items across the same 12 regions, plus **hair and hairline, facial hair/beard, and teeth** as named regions; publish one count and keep it consistent | P0 | Match |
| 2 | Demographic norms | Textbook ranges by sex, a few ethnicity rows incl. Middle Eastern, no SD or n [F] | **Gulf Arab (Emirati, Saudi), Levantine, Persian, South Asian and other expatriate norm sets** by sex and age band, shown as mean ± SD, n and source; collect proprietary consented local data; show a percentile within the user's own group *and* a global reference | P0 | Exceed |
| 3 | Measurement library | About 65 templated public pages, author–year citations, unnamed reviewer [F] | Same template plus DOIs, a named reviewer and date per page, a **measurement-error figure**, and Arabic and English versions | P1 | Exceed |

Two of these items are product bets rather than parity work. Gulf-stratified norms (item 2) are the strongest moat available. Qoves' Middle Eastern rows are a single line in a few tables, and the cross-cultural literature finds averageness and dimorphism weaker predictors across populations than Western studies suggest ([PMC10427909](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10427909/)). Building local reference data with consent is therefore both scientifically defensible and hard to copy. Body analysis (item 7) is the second bet. Qoves has nothing there, but it is also where the risk of eating disorders and body dysmorphia is highest. It should ship only after the safety screen (item 5) and the opt-in scoring (item 10) are in place.

**Open questions before building.** Several facts could not be confirmed. Qoves' live price and add-on prices could not be checked at checkout, its current 18-section report outline is unknown, its paid-ad creative is unknown, and the 2026 Guardian article was not read in full. UAE and KSA advertising, health-claim and data-protection rules were outside this study's scope and need a dedicated legal review.

## Conclusion

Qoves' moat is not its science. Its norms are borrowed, its reviewers are anonymous and its own numbers do not agree. The moat is a five-year-old YouTube audience and a writing style that makes people feel understood feature by feature. A Gulf competitor cannot outspend that audience at launch. It can make the write-up verifiably personal and regionally calibrated, with every sentence anchored to the user's own measurement and a norm group that actually includes them. It can also put the clinician's name on the report Qoves leaves unsigned.

The wider lesson from the arXiv finding and five years of press criticism is that safety cannot be a disclaimer bolted on afterwards. Qoves' weakest points are the bypassable age gate, the surgery upsell inside a "non-surgical" product, and projections that age the customer. Each of those is where its revenue model and user wellbeing pull in opposite directions. A competitor that keeps scores opt-in, screens customers before upload, refunds after delivery and earns nothing from surgery gives up some upsell revenue. In return it gets the one thing critics have never granted Qoves: a credible claim to be the responsible choice in a category regulators are starting to watch.
